Most payment problems are decided before the project starts. If the agreement says "payment due on completion" and the project takes four months, you've agreed to be an interest-free lender for four months, and to have the whole fee at risk if something goes wrong.

Payment terms are part of the deal, so agree them in the proposal, not in an awkward email later. This guide covers the three decisions (deposit, milestones and terms) and gives plain-language wording you can adapt.

*This is general information, not legal advice. Contract and payment rules differ between countries and between business and consumer clients. Have a professional review your agreement template if you're unsure.*

## Why structure matters

Bonsai analyzed invoices from over 100,000 freelancers and found **29% were paid at least a day late**. Invoices over $20,000 were **three times more likely** to be late than invoices under $100 (Bonsai, updated January 2026). Bigger invoices carry more risk, so splitting a large fee into smaller, earlier payments reduces how much is outstanding at any moment.

## Decision 1: the deposit

A deposit (paid before work starts) does three things: it confirms the client is committed, it covers your early costs, and it tests the payment process before much is at stake.

**Common structures:**

| Project size | Typical structure (examples, not rules) |
|---|---|
| Small, short project | 50% upfront, 50% on delivery, or 100% upfront for very small jobs |
| Medium project | Deposit + one or two milestone payments + final payment |
| Long engagement | Monthly retainer, invoiced in advance |

Pick percentages that fit your costs and market. What matters is that they're written down and applied consistently.

**Wording:**

> A deposit of [X]% ([amount]) is due before work begins and secures your start date. Work starts once the deposit has cleared.

"Work starts once the deposit has cleared" is the sentence that does the work. Stick to it. Starting early teaches the client that your terms are optional.

## Decision 2: milestones

Milestone payments tie money to visible progress, which clients generally find fair.

Good milestones are:

- **Observable**: "Homepage design approved", not "50% complete"
- **Within the client's control to approve**, with a deadline for approval
- **Evenly spread**, so no single invoice carries most of the risk

**Wording:**

> Payments are due as follows: [X]% deposit before work begins; [Y]% when [milestone 1] is approved; [Z]% on final delivery. Each milestone is considered approved if we haven't received feedback within [N] business days of delivery.

That last clause (sometimes called deemed approval) prevents a project, and its payments, from stalling while feedback sits in someone's inbox. Adapt it to your situation and check it suits your jurisdiction.

## Decision 3: payment terms

"Payment terms" is how long the client has to pay each invoice.

- **Shorter terms** (e.g., due on receipt, 7 or 14 days) are common for small businesses and individual clients.
- **Longer terms** (30+ days) are common with larger companies, whose accounts-payable processes may not bend.

Whatever you choose:

- **Put an actual date on each invoice** ("Due 14 October 2026"), not just "Net 14". Dates are harder to miss.
- **Name the late-payment consequence** only if it's in your agreement and allowed where you work. In the UK, for instance, businesses can claim statutory interest on late commercial payments (see GOV.UK's guidance on invoicing and late payment). Other countries have different rules.
- **Ask who pays** during onboarding: the accounts contact, and whether a purchase order number is required.

## Putting it together: a payment terms section

Here's a complete example for a fictional $6,000 website project. Adapt the numbers.

> **Investment and payment**
> The project fee is $6,000, payable in three parts:
> - $2,000 deposit before work begins. Work starts once it has cleared.
> - $2,000 when the design is approved.
> - $2,000 on launch.
>
> Invoices are due within 14 days of the invoice date. Each stage is treated as approved if we haven't received feedback within 5 business days of delivery. Work outside the scope above is quoted separately before it begins.

Include it in your proposal (see [writing proposals with AI](/blog/write-a-proposal-with-ai/): write the Investment section yourself) and repeat it in your agreement.

## What if the client pushes back?

Some clients, especially larger ones, have fixed payment processes. Useful questions:

- "Is the deposit the issue, or the timing of the later payments?"
- "Would a smaller deposit with more milestones work better for your process?"
- "Is there a purchase order process I should know about so invoices aren't delayed?"

Flexibility on structure is fine. Flexibility on *whether* terms exist is where problems start.

## Where this fits in your system

- **Proposal:** payment terms in the Investment section
- **Onboarding:** deposit invoice sent with the agreement (see [the onboarding checklist](/blog/client-onboarding-checklist/))

**The takeaway:** decide your deposit, milestones and terms once, write them in plain language, and put them in every proposal. Most late-payment problems then don't happen.
