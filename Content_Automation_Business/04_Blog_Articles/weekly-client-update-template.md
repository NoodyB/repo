Most "just checking in" emails from clients mean one thing: they don't know what's happening. A short, predictable weekly update answers that question before it's asked. It also makes the harder conversations easier: when a delay or scope change comes up, it's one line in a message the client already expects, not a surprise.

Here's a four-part template, an AI prompt that drafts it from rough notes, and the habits that keep it to five minutes per client.

## The four-part template

Every update answers the same four questions, in the same order:

```text
Subject: [Project] – update for week of [date]

Hi [Name],

Done this week
- [Finished item, in plain language]
- [Finished item]

Next week
- [What you'll work on]
- [Milestone or date coming up]

Needed from you (by [date])
- [Specific decision, file, access or feedback]
- (or: "Nothing this week.")

Risks or changes
- [Anything that might affect the timeline or scope, stated plainly]
- (or: "None. We're on track for [milestone] on [date].")

Best,
[Your name]
```

That's it. No essay, no status-report theater. The **Needed from you** section does the most work. It turns vague delays ("waiting on the client") into specific requests with dates.

## Why weekly (and why the same day)

Agree the update day during onboarding (it's on the [client onboarding checklist](/blog/client-onboarding-checklist/)) and send it every week, including quiet weeks. Predictability is the point:

- Clients stop chasing you, because they know when news arrives.
- Problems surface early, while they're small.
- You get a written record of what was requested and when, which is useful if the timeline slips because feedback came late.

## Draft it from notes with AI

Keep a running note per client during the week (a line or two whenever something happens). On update day, paste the notes into this prompt:

```text
Turn my rough notes into a weekly client update using this structure: Done this week /
Next week / Needed from you (with dates) / Risks or changes. Notes (redacted):
"""[NOTES]""". Keep it under 180 words, in my voice [VOICE PROFILE]. If a delay is
mentioned, state it plainly with the new expected date and don't soften it into
vagueness. Don't invent reasons, dates or progress that aren't in my notes.
```

The last line is the important guardrail: the assistant should **format and clarify** what you wrote, not add progress that didn't happen. Replace the client's name and any confidential details with placeholders before pasting (see [keeping client data private with AI](/blog/client-data-privacy-ai/)).

If the result sounds stiff, the fix is a voice profile: a short description of how you write, built from a few of your real emails. [Here's how to create one](/blog/make-ai-sound-like-you/).

## How to report a delay

The hardest update to write is the one with bad news. A simple rule: **state it, explain it in one sentence, give the new date, and offer a choice if there is one.**

> **Risks or changes**
> The testing phase is taking longer than planned because two payment integrations behave differently on mobile. The new expected date for launch is Thursday 16 October (previously Monday 13 October). If the original date is essential, we can launch without the second payment option and add it the following week. Let me know which you prefer.

No over-apologizing, no vague "slight delays", and a decision the client can make.

## How to flag scope creep in an update

When a request falls outside the agreed scope, the update is a low-drama place to name it:

> **Needed from you**
> The new landing page you mentioned on Tuesday isn't part of the current scope. I can send a short quote for it by Friday, or we can add it to a later phase. Which would you prefer?

For fuller wording, see [scope creep emails that protect the relationship](/blog/scope-creep-email-templates/).

## Keep it to five minutes

- **Notes as you go.** Thirty seconds after each work session beats reconstructing the week on Friday.
- **One template, saved.** In your email client's templates or a text-expander snippet.
- **Batch it.** Send all client updates in one sitting during your [weekly admin review](/blog/weekly-admin-review/).
- **AI for the draft, you for the send.** Read every update before it goes. Check dates, names and anything the client might misread.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Sent on the agreed day
- [ ] "Needed from you" items have dates
- [ ] Delays include a new date
- [ ] Nothing claimed that didn't happen
- [ ] Under about 200 words

**The takeaway:** a weekly update is the cheapest client-retention tool you have. Four headings, five minutes, every week.
