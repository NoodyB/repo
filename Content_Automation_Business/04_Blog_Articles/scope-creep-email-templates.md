Scope creep rarely arrives as a big, obvious request. It arrives as "one small thing", "while you're in there", or a new idea mentioned in passing on a call. Each one is reasonable on its own. Together, they turn a profitable project into an unprofitable one, and quietly change what the client expects from you.

The goal isn't to say no. It's to make every change **visible** and give the client an easy choice. Here are four templates for the situations that come up most, plus the setup that makes them easier to send.

## First: make scope visible before the project starts

Scope conversations are much easier when the proposal already lists:

- **Deliverables** as a numbered list
- **What's not included**, specifically
- **How many revision rounds** are included
- **How changes are handled**: for example, "Changes outside this scope are quoted separately before work begins"

If you're using AI to draft proposals, [include an explicit "not included" section](/blog/write-a-proposal-with-ai/). It's the single most useful line when a change request arrives.

## Template 1: The small extra (you're happy to do it)

Doing a small favor is fine. Doing it *invisibly* is what causes trouble, because next time it's expected.

```text
Hi [Name],

Happy to add [small request]. It's a quick one, so there's no charge this time.
Just flagging that it's outside the original scope, so larger additions like this
would normally be quoted separately.

It'll be ready by [date].

[Your name]
```

You've been generous *and* you've reinforced the boundary.

## Template 2: The bigger request (quote it)

```text
Hi [Name],

Thanks for the idea about [request]. I think it could work well.

It's outside the current scope (which covers [one-line scope summary]), so here are
two options:

1. Add it to this project: [price / "I'll send a quote by [date]"], and the launch date
   moves to [new date / "I'll confirm"].
2. Keep the current launch date and handle it as a follow-up phase afterwards.

Which would you prefer? I'll hold off on this piece until I hear back.

[Your name]
```

The last line matters: **don't start the extra work until the client chooses.** Starting "just to be helpful" makes it very hard to charge for later.

## Template 3: Repeated changes to agreed work

When a deliverable keeps changing after sign-off, name the pattern, not the person.

```text
Hi [Name],

Quick process note before I make the latest changes to [deliverable].

We agreed [number] revision rounds, and this would be round [number]. I'm glad to keep
refining it. Additional rounds are [price per round / "quoted at my hourly rate of
[rate]"].

To keep things efficient, could you gather all feedback from your side into one list
by [date]? That way I can make every change in one pass.

[Your name]
```

Asking for **consolidated feedback** often solves the underlying problem: several people sending partial comments at different times.

## Template 4: The "quick favor" outside the project entirely

```text
Hi [Name],

I'd be glad to help with [favor]. It's separate from our current project, so I'd
handle it as a small standalone job at [price / rate]. I can start on [date].

Want me to go ahead?

[Your name]
```

Short and friendly, and it treats the request as the new piece of work it is.

## Where to raise scope changes

- **In writing, always.** A call is fine for discussing, but confirm the outcome by email so there's a record.
- **In the weekly update**, for smaller items. A "Needed from you" line such as "The new landing page isn't in the current scope: quote or later phase?" keeps it low-drama. See [the weekly client update template](/blog/weekly-client-update-template/).
- **Before the work, not after.** Invoicing for extra work the client didn't agree to price is where relationships and payments go wrong.

Payment risk is real: in Bonsai's data from over 100,000 freelancers, 29% of invoices were paid at least a day late (Bonsai, updated January 2026). Unagreed extras on an invoice give a client one more reason to delay. Agreed extras don't.

## Using AI for these emails

AI is useful for adjusting tone. It can make a firm message warmer or a rambling one shorter. A simple prompt:

```text
A client asked for something outside our agreed scope. Scope: """[SCOPE]""".
Their request: """[REQUEST]""". Draft a friendly, confident email that thanks them,
notes it's outside the agreed scope (without blaming), and offers two options:
(a) [PRICE / TIME IMPACT I'LL PROVIDE] or (b) park it for a later phase. Ask them
to confirm which before I start. Under 170 words, in my voice [VOICE PROFILE].
Don't invent prices or dates.
```

You supply the price and the time impact. The assistant only handles the wording.

## A checklist before you hit send

- [ ] Refers to the agreed scope specifically
- [ ] Offers a choice, not an ultimatum
- [ ] States any price or timeline impact clearly (numbers you chose)
- [ ] Says you'll wait for confirmation before starting
- [ ] Friendly, and without apologizing for having a scope

**The takeaway:** scope creep is a visibility problem. Name the change, offer a choice, wait for a yes. The templates above make that a two-minute job instead of an awkward one.
