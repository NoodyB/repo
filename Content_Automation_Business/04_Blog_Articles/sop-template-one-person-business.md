"SOP" sounds like something for companies with operations departments. But if you run a business on your own, you're the one person who has to remember how everything works: how you onboard clients, how you invoice, what you check before a launch. Every time you re-figure it out, you pay for it in time and small mistakes.

Admin time adds up. In a 2023 survey of 251 US entrepreneurs by Time etc, respondents reported spending an average of **36% of their work week on admin tasks**. A standard operating procedure (SOP) is simply a written checklist for a recurring process, so you decide how to do it **once**.

This guide gives you a lightweight SOP template, tells you which processes to document first, and includes an AI prompt that turns rough notes into a finished SOP.

## Why solo businesses need SOPs

- **Fewer decisions.** You follow the checklist instead of reconstructing the process.
- **Fewer mistakes.** The step you forgot last time is written down this time.
- **Easier delegation later.** If you ever hire a VA or subcontractor, the SOP *is* the training.
- **Better automation.** You can't automate a process you haven't defined (see [what to automate first](/blog/what-to-automate-first/)).

## What to document first

Start with processes that are **frequent**, **multi-step** and **costly when you get them wrong**. For most service businesses:

1. **Client onboarding**, from "yes" to kickoff (see the [onboarding checklist](/blog/client-onboarding-checklist/))
2. **Invoicing and payment follow-up**
3. **Project delivery and handover**
4. **The weekly admin review** (see [the 45-minute agenda](/blog/weekly-admin-review/))
5. **Anything you do monthly or quarterly**, because those are the ones you forget between runs

## The SOP template

Keep SOPs short. If one runs over a page, split it.

```text
SOP name:        [e.g., "New client onboarding"]
Purpose:         [One sentence: why this process exists]
Trigger:         [What starts it: "Client accepts proposal"]
Time:            [Typical minutes]
Tools & templates: [Links to the templates, folders and tools used]

Steps:
1. [Verb-first step: "Duplicate the client folder template"]
2. [...]
(max 12 steps; if more, split into two SOPs)

Done when:       [The observable finish line: "Deposit paid and kickoff booked"]
Common mistakes: [What went wrong before, and how to avoid it]
Last reviewed:   [Date]
```

Three details make SOPs usable:

- **Verb-first steps** ("Send", "Check", "Duplicate") are easy to follow when you're tired.
- **"Done when"** stops processes drifting on without an end.
- **"Common mistakes"** turns past problems into future prevention. It's the most valuable section.

## Example: invoicing SOP (fictional)

```text
SOP name:        Milestone invoicing
Purpose:         Invoice promptly and correctly so payment isn't delayed
Trigger:         Client approves a milestone
Time:            10 minutes
Tools:           Invoicing tool (template "Milestone"), invoice tracker sheet

Steps:
1. Open the client's agreement and confirm the milestone amount.
2. Duplicate the "Milestone" invoice template.
3. Enter the next invoice number (tracker, column A).
4. Write the description: milestone name + approval date.
5. Set the due date as an actual date (terms from the agreement).
6. Add the PO number if the client requires one.
7. Send with the invoice cover email template.
8. Log it in the tracker with the due date.

Done when:       Invoice sent and logged with a due date
Common mistakes: Forgetting the PO number (client X's accounts team rejects
                 invoices without one); using "Net 14" instead of a date
Last reviewed:   2026-09-24
```

## Turn rough notes into an SOP with AI

The fastest way to write an SOP is to do the process once while jotting rough notes, then let an AI assistant format them:

```text
Turn my rough notes into an SOP using this template: SOP name, Purpose, Trigger,
Time, Tools & templates, Steps (numbered, each starting with a verb, max 12), Done
when, Common mistakes, Last reviewed [TODAY'S DATE]. Notes: """[NOTES]""". Keep my
exact tool names and links. Where a step is unclear or seems to be missing, add
[CHECK: question] instead of guessing.
```

The `[CHECK: question]` instruction is important. It makes the assistant flag gaps rather than invent steps you don't actually do. Review every SOP it produces, and delete anything that isn't true to how you work.

Don't paste client names or confidential details into the notes. SOPs should describe the *process*, not a specific client.

## Where to keep SOPs

Anywhere you'll actually open them:

- A folder of documents with a simple index
- A notes app or wiki (Notion, Obsidian, OneNote)
- A spreadsheet index listing each SOP, its link and when it was last reviewed

The index matters more than the tool. You need to see all your SOPs in one list, and which ones are overdue for a review.

## Keep them alive

- **Review quarterly**, or whenever a step changes.
- **Update after mistakes.** If something went wrong, add it to "Common mistakes" the same day.
- **One improvement a week.** During your [weekly admin review](/blog/weekly-admin-review/), turn one annoying repeated task into an SOP, template or saved prompt.

**The takeaway:** an SOP is a checklist with a purpose, a trigger and a finish line. Start with onboarding and invoicing, keep each one to a page, and let AI handle the formatting while you handle the truth.
