# How to use this playbook

You run a service business on your own. You're good at the work clients pay for. You're less excited about everything around it: replying to inquiries, writing proposals, onboarding, status updates, invoices, follow-ups, and the weekly scramble to remember who needs what.

This playbook gives you a **complete, practical system** for that admin, using AI where it genuinely helps and simple templates where AI would be overkill. It is built for people who don't want another subscription and don't want to become automation engineers.

## What you'll have when you finish

- **An admin map** of your business: every recurring task, how long it takes, and what to do about it.
- **An AI workspace set up safely**: a reusable business brief, a voice profile, and privacy habits that protect your clients.
- **Six client workflows**, each with a checklist, templates and prompts: inquiries and discovery, proposals, onboarding, delivery communication, getting paid, and offboarding.
- **A starter SOP library** so you stop reinventing your own processes.
- **A 45-minute weekly admin review** that keeps the whole system running.

## How long it takes

Plan on **about three hours spread across a week**. The final section, *Your next seven days*, is a day-by-day implementation plan. You don't need to finish everything before you benefit: the onboarding and getting-paid workflows can be used the same day you read them.

# The admin problem in a business of one

!!! tip "In this chapter"
    Why admin swallows so much of a solo business's week, what the evidence actually says, and the three levels of fixing it (only one of which involves "automation").

## Admin is a real cost, not a character flaw

If admin feels like it's eating your week, you are not imagining it. In a survey of 251 U.S. entrepreneurs commissioned by the virtual-assistant company Time etc (September 2023), respondents reported spending **an average of 36% of their work week on administrative tasks**. In a typical week, 44% said they create invoices and 27% chase late payers.^[1]^

Late payment is a measurable problem too. The freelance software company Bonsai analyzed invoicing data from more than 100,000 freelancers over three years and found that **29% of invoices were paid at least one day late**. Larger invoices were more likely to be late than small ones.^[2]^

Both sources are companies with something to sell, so treat the exact numbers as directional. But the pattern matches what solo business owners say in public communities again and again: the work is fine; the *wrapping* around the work is exhausting.

## Most small businesses haven't cracked AI yet, and that's an advantage

The U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey found that between December 2025 and May 2026, overall business AI use "hovered between 17% and 20%", and **fewer than 20% of firms with four or fewer employees reported using AI**. Use among firms with fewer than 20 employees didn't change significantly over that period.^[3]^

In other words, if you set up a sensible AI-assisted system now, you're early, not late.

## The three levels of fixing admin

Most "automate your business" advice jumps straight to complicated tools. In a business of one, the biggest wins usually come from the first two levels:

| Level | What it is | Typical examples | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Decide once** | Templates, checklists and standard answers, with no AI needed | Onboarding checklist; standard payment terms; saved email replies | Low |
| **2. Draft with AI** | AI turns your notes into a first draft that you edit | Proposals from call notes; weekly updates; meeting summaries; SOPs | Low–medium |
| **3. Automate** | Software triggers actions without you | Form → spreadsheet → auto-reply; invoice reminders sent by your invoicing tool | Medium |

**The rule of this book:** move a task up a level only when the level below it is working. A great template beats a flaky automation every time.

## What AI is (and isn't) good at in your admin

| AI is good at | AI is bad at, or risky for |
|---|---|
| Turning messy notes into clear drafts | Knowing facts about your client it wasn't told |
| Matching a tone you describe or demonstrate | Making commitments on your behalf |
| Summarizing long threads and transcripts | Legal, tax or jurisdiction-specific advice |
| Producing variations (subject lines, options) | Anything that needs judgment about a relationship |
| Checking your draft against a checklist | Handling confidential data you haven't cleared to share |

!!! warning "The golden rule"
    AI drafts; you decide. Nothing AI writes goes to a client until you've read it, fixed it, and would be comfortable signing it with your own name. You would be.

# Run your admin audit

!!! tip "In this chapter"
    A 30-minute exercise that shows exactly where your admin time goes and which tasks to fix first. Everything else in the book builds on it.

## Step 1: List every recurring admin task

Use the prompts below to jog your memory. Aim for 10–25 tasks. Include anything you do more than once a month that isn't the paid work itself.

- **Getting clients:** replying to inquiries, discovery calls, proposals, follow-ups, contracts.
- **Starting projects:** welcome emails, questionnaires, folders, kickoff scheduling, deposits.
- **During projects:** status updates, meeting notes, feedback rounds, scope questions, file sharing.
- **Money:** invoices, payment reminders, receipts, expense logging, price quotes.
- **Finishing:** handover documents, testimonial requests, referrals, archiving.
- **Running the business:** scheduling, social posts, newsletter, bookkeeping prep, tool subscriptions.

## Step 2: Estimate frequency and minutes

For each task, write how many times a month you do it and roughly how many minutes it takes each time. Estimates are fine. You'll refine them later.

## Step 3: Score repeatability and risk

- **Repeatability (1–3):** 3 = almost identical every time; 2 = similar with some variation; 1 = different every time.
- **Risk (1–3):** 1 = low stakes (a mistake is easy to fix); 2 = medium (money, client data, deadlines); 3 = high (legal, sensitive or relationship-critical).

## Step 4: Calculate the priority score

> **Priority score = (times per month × minutes each) × repeatability ÷ risk**

High scores mean frequent, time-consuming, repeatable and low-risk tasks: the best candidates to fix first. *The Client Desk* workbook calculates this for you on its **Admin Audit** tab.

## Step 5: Choose a decision for each task

| Decision | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Eliminate** | Nobody would notice if you stopped (e.g., a report no client reads) |
| **Template** | The task is nearly identical every time |
| **AI draft** | The structure repeats but the content changes (proposals, updates) |
| **Automate** | It's rule-based, frequent, low-risk, and already templated |
| **Keep human** | High risk, high judgment, or relationship-critical |

!!! example "Worked example: Sam's audit (fictional)"
    Sam is a **fictional** freelance web designer we'll follow through the book. Sam has four active clients. Sam's first audit:

| Task | Times/mo | Min each | Min/mo | Repeat (1–3) | Risk (1–3) | Priority | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly client updates | 16 | 20 | 320 | 3 | 1 | **960** | Template + AI draft |
| Social media posts | 8 | 30 | 240 | 2 | 1 | 480 | AI-assisted repurposing |
| Replying to inquiries | 8 | 15 | 120 | 3 | 1 | 360 | Template + AI draft |
| Onboarding emails & setup | 2 | 60 | 120 | 3 | 1 | 360 | Checklist + templates |
| Writing proposals | 3 | 90 | 270 | 2 | 2 | 270 | AI draft from call notes |
| Meeting notes & follow-ups | 12 | 15 | 180 | 3 | 2 | 270 | AI summary (redacted) |
| Sending invoices | 6 | 15 | 90 | 3 | 2 | 135 | Template in invoicing tool |
| Chasing late payments | 3 | 20 | 60 | 3 | 2 | 90 | Reminder sequence |
| **Total** |  |  | **1,400 (≈23 h)** |  |  |  |  |

Sam's biggest surprise: the "small" weekly updates add up to more time than proposals. They also score highest, because they're identical in structure and low-risk. That's where Sam starts.

!!! note "About time savings"
    We can't promise how much time you'll save. It depends on your clients, tools and habits. Measure instead: re-estimate the minutes for your top five tasks after two weeks of using the new workflows, and compare.

# Set up your AI workspace safely

!!! tip "In this chapter"
    Pick an assistant, protect client confidentiality, and create two reusable documents (your **business brief** and your **voice profile**) that make every AI draft sound more like you.

## Pick one assistant and stick with it

For admin work, the major general-purpose assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot) are all capable. Free tiers are enough to start. Choose based on what you already use: if your email and documents live in Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, the matching assistant may integrate more smoothly. What matters most is using **one** assistant consistently so your saved instructions and habits build up.

## Protect your clients' information

Before you paste anything about a client into an AI tool, do three things once:

1. **Check your settings.** Most assistants let you control whether your conversations are used to improve their models. In ChatGPT, this is the **"Improve the model for everyone"** toggle under **Settings → Data controls**; when it's off, new conversations aren't used for training.^[4]^ Anthropic's consumer Claude plans let you choose whether chats are used to improve Claude, and you can change it any time in **Privacy Settings**.^[5]^ Other assistants have similar controls. Look them up and make a deliberate choice.
2. **Check your client agreements.** Some contracts and NDAs restrict sharing client information with third-party services. If yours do, use redaction (below) or don't use AI for that client's material.
3. **Adopt a redaction habit.** Replace names, company names, email addresses, phone numbers, financial details and anything sensitive with placeholders before you paste.

!!! checklist "Redaction checklist, before you paste"
    - [ ] Client and company names → `[CLIENT]`, `[COMPANY]`
    - [ ] Emails, phone numbers, addresses → removed
    - [ ] Bank, card or tax ID numbers → never pasted
    - [ ] Health, legal or HR details about individuals → removed
    - [ ] Passwords, API keys, login links → never pasted
    - [ ] Anything under NDA → removed, or AI not used

!!! prompt "SET-03 · Redaction check before pasting"
    I'm about to paste text into an AI assistant. Act as a careful privacy reviewer. Scan the text between the triple quotes and list every item that could identify a person or company or expose sensitive data (names, emails, phone numbers, addresses, account numbers, prices tied to named clients, health/legal/HR details, passwords or links with tokens). Then return a redacted version that replaces each item with a clear placeholder like [CLIENT NAME] or [INVOICE AMOUNT]. Don't change anything else.
    """[PASTE TEXT]"""

*Tip: if you run this check inside the same assistant, the text has already been shared with it. Use it to catch things you missed while you learn the habit, or run it on a version you've already partly redacted.*

## Create your business brief (once, reuse forever)

AI drafts are generic when the AI knows nothing about you. A **business brief** is a one-page description you paste at the start of a conversation, or save into your assistant's custom instructions, project or "memory" feature.

!!! prompt "SET-01 · Business brief builder"
    Help me write a one-page "business brief" I can give an AI assistant before asking for help with client admin. Interview me with one question at a time (wait for each answer) covering: what I do and for whom; my 2–4 core services and typical project size; how I like to work (communication hours, response times, meeting preferences); my payment terms and policies; phrases or promises I never want used; and three examples of great client outcomes (described generally, no client names). When we're done, output the brief in this format: ## Who I am, ## Services, ## Ideal clients, ## How I work, ## Policies, ## Tone and words to avoid. Keep it under 450 words.

!!! example "Excerpt from Sam's business brief (fictional)"
    **Who I am:** Independent web designer (Squarespace and Webflow) for local professional-service firms.
    **How I work:** Replies within one business day; client calls Tue–Thu; one round of consolidated feedback per milestone.
    **Policies:** 50% deposit to start; invoices due in 14 days; out-of-scope requests quoted separately before work begins.
    **Tone:** Friendly, direct, no jargon. Never promise launch dates before content is received.

## Create your voice profile

Your clients hired *you*, so your emails should still sound like you. Collect **three to five emails you've written that you're happy with** (redacted), and ask your assistant to describe your style.

!!! prompt "SET-02 · Voice profile from writing samples"
    Below are [NUMBER] emails I wrote to clients (names removed). Analyze my writing style and produce a "voice profile" I can reuse in future prompts. Include: typical sentence length; greeting and sign-off habits; level of formality (1–5); words and phrases I use often; things I never do (e.g., exclamation marks, emojis, corporate phrases); how I deliver bad news; and 3 short "do this / not that" examples. Keep it under 250 words and don't invent traits that aren't in the samples.
    """[PASTE 3–5 REDACTED EMAILS]"""

Save both documents in one place. From now on, the most effective prompt starter is:

> *"Here is my business brief and voice profile: [paste]. Using them, …"*

## The four-check review (use on every draft)

Before any AI-drafted message leaves your outbox, check:

1. **Facts:** Are dates, prices, names and deliverables correct?
2. **Promises:** Did the draft commit you to anything you didn't intend (deadlines, extra work, guarantees)?
3. **Tone:** Does it sound like you, and like how you treat this client?
4. **Specifics:** Does it mention something only *this* client would recognize? If not, add one line that does.
